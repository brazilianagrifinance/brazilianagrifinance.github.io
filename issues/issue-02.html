The Private Stack That Replaced the State

Brazil's harvest is no longer financed by the government. A R$1.41 trillion private credit architecture — built on CPRs, CRAs, and FIAGROs — has quietly displaced the Plano Safra as the sector's dominant funding source. Here is how the stack works, who sits where in the risk structure, and what breaks first when a cycle turns.

The Plano Safra — Brazil's official government credit program for agriculture — covers roughly one-third of the sector's actual financing needs. The rest is absorbed by a private architecture that most international allocators can name but few can navigate with precision. Understanding that architecture is a prerequisite for understanding almost everything else about Brazilian agri finance: where risk accumulates, how contagion travels, and why a soybean farmer's payment deadline in April matters to a FIAGRO investor in São Paulo.

This edition maps the stack — not as a glossary, but as a risk diagram.

The four instruments and where they sit

The Brazilian Agro Finance Stack — from farm gate to capital market
CPR
Cédula de Produto Rural
The foundation. A promise of future delivery of agricultural product — or its cash equivalent — issued directly by the producer or cooperative. Created in 1994, the CPR is the instrument that ended the state's monopoly on rural credit. It sits at the farm gate: the producer issues a CPR to a trading company, input supplier, or financial institution in exchange for upfront financing.
Stock (Mar 2026): R$560B  ·  Issuer: Producer / Cooperative  ·  Two types: Physical (grain delivery) and Financial (cash settlement)
LCA
Letra de Crédito do Agronegócio
Bank-issued bonds backed by agro credit portfolios. The LCA is the retail entry point into agro credit — tax-exempt for individual investors, widely distributed by Brazilian banks. The bank originates rural credit, packages it into an LCA, and sells to retail investors. The risk sits with the bank, not the producer. Large stock, but lower analytical relevance for institutional allocators.
Stock (2025): R$603B  ·  Issuer: Financial institutions  ·  Investor: Primarily retail, tax-exempt
CRA
Certificado de Recebíveis do Agronegócio
Asset-backed securities structured by securitization companies and backed by agro receivables — CPRs, CDCAs, or commercial contracts. The CRA is the instrument that connected the agro sector to the capital market. A trading company or large producer issues a debenture, which a securitizer uses as collateral to issue a CRA to institutional investors. The credit risk travels from the obligor (producer or trader) through the structure to the investor.
Stock (2025): R$173B  ·  Issuer: Securitizers (Vert, True, Eco)  ·  Backed by: Debêntures most common (R$57.8B)
FIAGRO
Fundo de Investimento Agro
Listed funds that invest across the agro credit stack — primarily CRAs, but also CDCAs, real estate, and equity. Created in 2021, FIAGROs grew from zero to R$56B AUM in under four years. Three subtypes: FIAGRO-FIDC (credit), FIAGRO-FII (real estate), and FIAGRO-FIP (equity). Listed on B3, which creates daily mark-to-market — and the sector's most visible signal of stress.
AUM (Mar 2026): R$56B  ·  Funds operating: 221  ·  Investor: Institutional + retail via B3

How the money flows from capital market to farm gate

The standard origination chain runs in the opposite direction from what most international allocators assume. Capital doesn't flow from investor to farmer directly — it flows through a series of intermediaries, each of which takes a spread and passes the risk along. Understanding that chain is essential for knowing where the stress appears first when a cycle turns.

Key implication
The investor's credit risk is to the originator, not the farmer. But the originator's risk is concentrated in farm-level CPRs. When a producer defaults on a CPR, it travels up the chain — from originator balance sheet to CRA structure to FIAGRO NAV — often faster than the transparency of the structure suggests.

The barter loop — where trading companies sit

The most operationally important financing mechanism in the Mato Grosso basin is the barter operation — and the least discussed in international research. In a barter transaction, an input supplier (fertilizers, seeds, agrochemicals) delivers inputs to the producer at planting time, and receives a CPR as payment: a promise of physical grain delivery after harvest. The input supplier then sells that CPR to a trading company, which becomes the creditor of the future grain.

This mechanism has three consequences that matter for capital allocators. First, it ties the producer's input financing directly to his origination calendar — if he can't deliver grain, the whole structure unwinds. Second, it gives the trading company a low-cost inventory mechanism: they fund inputs, receive grain, and sell FOB Santos without ever having to buy spot in the physical market. Third, it creates a population of CPR obligations that mature at harvest — which is exactly why CPR payment deadlines in April are a catalyst for farmer selling, as documented in our previous issue.

How the market learned about risk: the Agrogalaxy episode

On September 18, 2024, Agrogalaxy Participações — a consolidator of agricultural input distributors — filed for recuperação judicial with R$4.1 billion in total debt. Of that, approximately R$830 million were CRAs. The filing triggered immediate mark-to-market losses in at least eleven FIAGROs, with CPTR11 (Capitânia), XPCA11 (XP), and JGPX11 (JGP) each carrying exposures of 7–8% of NAV.

What happened next
CPTR11 zeroed dividends in October 2024 and provisioned 50% of its Agrogalaxy exposure (R$14.3M impact). FIAGROs with exposure fell 6–7% on the day of the filing. R$300M in CRA exposure across 11 funds became illiquid overnight as secondary market trading was suspended.
Source: XP Research, Brazil Journal, InfoMoney · September–October 2024

The Agrogalaxy episode established three things that are now priced into how sophisticated investors approach this market. First, concentration risk in single obligors is severe — even well-managed funds carried exposures that could zero a month's dividends. Second, the FIAGRO structure's B3 listing creates a liquidity mismatch: the underlying assets (CRAs, CDCAs) are illiquid, but the units trade daily. When retail investors panic and sell, NAV gaps open. Third, contagion between FIAGROs is real — because multiple funds held the same CRA, the stress was simultaneous and correlated.

The market did not collapse. But it restructured. New issuances in 2025 reflected tighter covenants, higher spreads for non-investment-grade obligors, and a clearer distinction between high-grade originator exposure and farm-level CPR risk. FIAGROs with diversified portfolios and large-trader obligors recovered strongly — HGAG11 gained 63.5% in 2025. Farm-level and single-obligor structures did not.

The imbalance
The stack totals R$1.41T — larger than Brazil's official rural credit program. But the CPR layer (R$560B), which carries the most direct farm-level risk, grew 28% YoY — the fastest of any instrument. Growth is concentrated where transparency is lowest and collateral quality is hardest to assess.
Source: MAPA (Mar 2026) · CVM Boletim CRA (2025) · B3 · ANBIMA · CNN Brasil Agro

The aggregate growth numbers tell a story of a maturing market. The risk distribution within that market tells a more nuanced one. The instruments with the most retail penetration — LCA (R$603B) and listed FIAGROs — have the clearest mark-to-market and the most regulatory oversight. The instruments with the most direct farm exposure — CPRs (R$560B) — have the least. The CPR market grew 28% year-over-year through March 2026, the fastest pace of any instrument in the stack. That growth reflects genuine demand for pre-financing from producers, but it also means the highest-risk layer of the architecture is expanding fastest.

The regulatory shift to watch
The 2024 Resolução CVM 214 introduced formal regulation for FIAGROs for the first time — previously operating under temporary rules. Funds had until September 2025 to adapt. Separately, a proposed tax reform veto would subject FIAGROs to IBS/CBS, which could alter the product's tax economics for retail investors. The regulatory trajectory matters: tighter oversight raises the quality floor but may constrain smaller issuers.
So what — implications by reader type
For the commodity fund researcher
  • The CPR layer is your leading indicator of farm-level stress — not FIAGRO NAVs, which lag. A spike in CPR renegotiations or missed deliveries precedes FIAGRO mark-downs by 30–60 days.
  • The Agrogalaxy episode showed that FIAGRO correlation is high — multiple funds holding the same CRA means stress is simultaneous. In your model, treat concentrated FIAGRO exposure to a single obligor as a binary risk, not a spread risk.
  • Watch the FIAGRO-FIDC subtype: it carries the most direct credit exposure and the least liquidity buffer. FIAGRO-FII (real estate) is structurally more insulated from commodity cycle stress.
For the physical trader / originator
  • Barter CPRs maturing at harvest are the mechanism connecting the capital market to your origination calendar. When CPR payment deadlines cluster (as in April 2026), expect forced selling regardless of price — that is flow supply, not price-driven supply.
  • The trading company's balance sheet sits between the producer's CPR and the CRA structure. If you are long physical beans and short capital via CRAs, a producer default on a barter CPR hits your inventory and your funding simultaneously. Size your CRA-funded inventory positions with this correlation in mind.
For the credit / structured finance allocator
  • The three-tier differentiation that emerged post-Agrogalaxy is now the correct framework: (1) large trader / cooperative obligors with diversified CPR portfolios — investment grade risk; (2) mid-size input distributors with concentrated farm exposure — high yield with meaningful binary tail; (3) direct farm-level CPR structures — not institutional credit.
  • Belagrícola and Cotribá (both showing stress in late 2025) confirm the pattern: the mid-tier distributor is the structural weak point of the stack. Exposure to this segment via CRA deserves a materially higher spread than current market pricing implies.
  • Short-duration CRA with investment-grade originator obligors is the highest risk-adjusted position in Brazilian agro credit today. Long-duration farm-level structures are not. The market is not fully pricing this distinction yet.
Risk signals — what to watch in the stack
CPR renegotiation uptick
Leading indicator of farm-level stress. Watch MAPA's CPR registry data monthly — a volume increase in extensions precedes FIAGRO mark-downs by 30–60 days.
FIAGRO secondary discount widens
Listed FIAGROs trading at >5% discount to NAV signals retail panic or credit event uncertainty. Monitor CPTR11, XPCA11, JGPX11 as the most liquid reference points.
Mid-tier distributor stress
Belagrícola / Cotribá pattern: extrajudicial recovery followed by CRA cross-default. Any announcement of covenant breach by a mid-size input distributor is a read-through to CRA portfolios with farm-level exposure.
Regulatory IBS/CBS veto outcome
If Congress overturns the presidential veto, FIAGROs become liable for new consumption taxes — changing the product's tax economics for retail investors and likely slowing follow-on issuances in 2026.
